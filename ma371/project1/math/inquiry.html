Finding a Defensible Split in a Collaboration Graph

Guided inquiry and individual writing assignment

Work collaboratively during class; write the final memo and annex independently. Record your reasoning in the worksheet PDF or your own notes. The graph laboratory lets you test each mathematical step after making a prediction.

The decision: a program director must place six mathematical-sciences research teams in two seminar cohorts of three. Keeping teams with documented collaborations together may help them exchange ideas. Decide whether to retain the office's draft split or recommend another, and state what additional evidence could change your advice. All team and edge data are synthetic.

Course connections: Lesson 17, eigenvalues and eigenvectors; Lesson 18, diagonalization. Adjacency matrices, degree matrices, Laplacians, and graph cuts are introduced here.

1. Learn the graph objects

A graph has vertices (teams) and edges (documented two-team collaborations). Edges are undirected: {i,j} and {j,i} are the same tie. A simple graph has no self-edge and at most one edge for each pair.

Start with the three-vertex path having edges {1,2} and {2,3}. Its matrices are:

Adjacency matrix A
0 1 0 1 0 1 0 1 0
Degree matrix D
1 0 0 0 2 0 0 0 1
Laplacian L = D − A
1 -1 0 -1 2 -1 0 -1 1

For a simple undirected graph, Aij = 1 exactly when vertices i and j are joined, and 0 otherwise. The degree di counts neighbors; D puts degrees on its diagonal. Thus Lii = di, while an off-diagonal Lij is −1 for an edge and 0 otherwise.

  1. Why is A symmetric with a zero diagonal?
  2. Verify the second row of L directly from the path.
  3. Calculate L times the all-ones vector. Why do all Laplacians made this way have row sum zero?

Try the blank graph manipulative: choose three vertices and establish the two path edges yourself.

2. Build the six-team case

The recorded collaborations are E = {{1,2}, {1,3}, {2,3}, {4,5}, {4,6}, {5,6}, {2,5}, {3,4}}. The office draft places {1,2,5} in one cohort and {3,4,6} in the other. One edge means at least one documented collaboration; it does not measure its strength or productivity.

  1. Draw the six labeled vertices and eight edges by hand. Mark the office split.
  2. Build the 6 × 6 adjacency matrix in vertex order 1 through 6. Verify symmetry and the zero diagonal.
  3. Find the degree vector and D. Why is the sum of the degrees twice the number of edges?
  4. Construct L = D − A. Work out rows 1 and 4, then check the row sums.
  5. For any vector z, explain why the i-th coordinate of Lz equals the sum of zi − zj over neighbors j of i.

Load the six-team case, then use the degree and Laplacian actions separately. Predict before clicking each one.

3. Interpret eigen-information

For the three-vertex path, the Laplacian has eigenvalues 0, 1, and 3, with respective eigenvectors (1,1,1)T, (1,0,−1)T, and (1,−2,1)T. Verify one pair by multiplication. Because these independent eigenvectors form a basis, L = PΛP−1, with their eigenvalues on Λ's diagonal in matching order.

For the six-team case, the supplied Laplacian eigenvalues are 0, 1, 3, 3, 4, 5. An eigenvector for 0 is (1,1,1,1,1,1)T; one for the smallest positive eigenvalue, 1, is (2,1,1,−1,−1,−2)T. You are not expected to solve a degree-six characteristic equation by hand. Check and interpret the result.

For an undirected graph, the Laplacian is symmetric and has a real eigenvector basis. The number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components. The smallest-positive-eigenvalue eigenvector often suggests a candidate grouping by the signs of its coordinates. Its sign is arbitrary: multiplying the entire vector by −1 merely swaps the group labels.

The identity zTLz = Σ{i,j}∈E(zi−zj)2 measures variation across edges. The sign rule seeks a relatively smooth nonconstant pattern, but it does not prove that a particular balanced cut is optimal.

  1. Verify rows 1 and 4 of Lv = v.
  2. What do the zero eigenvalue and graph drawing together say about connectivity?
  3. Which groups do the eigenvector signs suggest? What would a coordinate near zero make ambiguous?
  4. In L = PΛP−1, what do P's columns and Λ's entries mean? Why does the repeated eigenvalue 3 not prevent diagonalization here?

Ask Python for the eigen-information only after making your own interpretation. The displayed vector is unit length, so its decimals may differ from the integer-scaled vector above while representing the same eigenpair.

4. Define a success metric and test the split

A balanced split S | Sc has three vertices on each side. The cut count C(S) is the number of recorded edges with endpoints in different cohorts. Of the eight recorded edges, 8 − C(S) remain within cohorts. This is a count of documented ties, not a probability of future success.

  1. Predict whether the office draft or the eigenvector-sign split cuts fewer edges.
  2. For each split, list crossing edges, count them, and compute the within-cohort count. Create one more balanced split of your own.
  3. Does the sign split improve this metric? Does the eigenvector equation alone prove it is the best possible split?
  4. Why would calling (8 − C(S))/8 the “chance of successful collaboration” be misleading?

Assign cohorts on the graph and calculate each cut separately. The orange crossing edges are a visual aid; the auditable edge list and exact integer count are the evidence.

5. Challenge the record

Suppose an audit finds two plausible but unrecorded ties: {1,5} and {3,6}. Compare the original eight-edge record, a record with only {1,5} added, and a record with both ties added. Hold the original office and sign-based partitions fixed when recounting; do not silently treat an old eigenvector as an eigenvector of the altered graph.

  1. For each record, recount the office and sign-split cuts. What happens to the original recommendation when both edges are present?
  2. What does the binary-edge model omit? Name information that could reverse your advice even if the edge list is complete.
  3. Why are these Laplacian eigenvalues not growth rates or week-by-week forecasts?

Use the stress-test edge controls and preserve earlier runs in the comparison table. A changed graph requires a fresh calculation if you want new eigen-information.

6. Write an independently authored Army memo

Before leaving class, note your provisional claim, mathematical evidence, limitation or counterevidence, and information needed next. A well-supported conditional recommendation is acceptable.

  • Memo: at most two pages. Use the Math Sci memo template addressed to the program director. State the decision criterion, numerical comparison, and a concrete condition or limitation in language a nontechnical leader can use.
  • Separate technical annex: at most three pages. Show the edge-to-matrix construction, selected eigenpair checks and interpretation, crossing-edge lists, and stress-test result. Make the arithmetic auditable; do not submit a calculator export as a substitute for explanation.

The memo and annex must be your own writing without generative AI outlining, drafting, rewriting, editing, or caption generation. If you used AI during class, disclose its limited role and your verification. The supplied calculator and its numerical output are permitted. Due date and submission method will be supplied by your instructor.
